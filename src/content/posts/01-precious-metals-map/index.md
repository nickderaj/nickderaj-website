## Overview

Six precious metals — gold, silver, palladium, platinum, ruthenium and iridium — underpin everything from jewellery and investment to catalytic converters, electronics, hydrogen fuel cells and chemical catalysis. Unlike oil, whose reserves are spread across dozens of producers, several of these metals are strikingly concentrated in just one or two countries.

This map lets you explore **known reserves** for each metal and toggle to see where the **active mines** are located.

## Gold

Gold reserves are the most geographically dispersed of the six metals shown here. **Australia** leads with roughly 12,000 tonnes of known reserves, followed by **Russia**, **South Africa**, **the United States** and **Peru**. Active mines span every inhabited continent, from Nevada to Papua New Guinea.

## Silver

**Peru** holds the world's largest silver reserves at approximately 98,000 tonnes, followed by **Australia** and **Poland** (home to the KGHM copper-silver deposits). Silver is often produced as a by-product of copper, lead and zinc mining, which means its supply is partly tied to base-metal economics.

## Platinum Group Metals

Palladium, platinum, ruthenium and iridium are all platinum group metals (PGMs). Their reserves are extraordinarily concentrated:

- **South Africa's Bushveld Complex** contains over 90% of the world's platinum and roughly 80% of ruthenium and iridium.
- **Russia's Norilsk-Talnakh** complex is the dominant source of palladium.
- **Zimbabwe's Great Dyke** is the only other significant PGM province.

This concentration makes PGM supply chains acutely sensitive to geopolitical and labour disruptions in a small number of regions.

Historically, Pt and Pd have been overwhelmingly used in **automotive exhaust after-treatment (autocatalysts)** since the 1970s, with jewellery and industrial catalysts as the next largest uses for Pt, and electronics and chemical catalysts for Pd. Ru became critical for **electronics (HDDs, chip resistors)** and **chemical/electrochemical catalysts**, while Ir is used mainly in **high-temperature components** and **electrochemical catalysts**, with fast-growing exposure to **PEM electrolysers and fuel cells**.

PGM autocatalyst demand (mainly Pt + Pd, with some Rh) rose from **2.2 Moz in 1990 to 13.8 Moz in 2019** — roughly a six-fold increase, even though global light-vehicle sales "only" rose from ~54m to ~92m. Within that, demand **shifted from Pt-heavy to Pd-heavy gasoline catalysts** in the 1990s–2000s, and is now swinging **back towards Pt** as Pd became expensive and tight. Ru and Ir were minor in 1990; demand has since grown multiple-fold with the **electronics boom** and now **hydrogen applications**, but they remain small in absolute tonnage compared with Pt and Pd.

USGS estimates **global PGM reserves at >81,000 tonnes**, with ~63,000 t in South Africa and 16,000 t in Russia. At current mining rates of roughly 400–450 t/year, the simple reserve-to-production ratio implies **~180–200 years of PGMs** for the group as a whole. Ru and Ir reserves are not broken out individually; their availability is about **processing capacity and price** rather than in-situ tonnes.

## Platinum

### Uses

Platinum's demand profile has shifted significantly over the decades. Before the 1970s, Pt was used primarily in **jewellery and chemical/glass labware**. The adoption of automotive emissions standards in the 1970s–1990s made autocatalysts the centre of gravity. Pt initially dominated both gasoline and diesel catalysts, but from the mid-1990s onward, **palladium displaced Pt in gasoline catalysts** on cost. Recent high Pd prices have pushed substitution back in Pt's favour.

Key uses today:

- **Autocatalysts** for diesel and gasoline vehicles, including heavy-duty trucks and hybrid powertrains
- **Industrial catalysts**: nitric acid, refinery hydrocracking, petrochemicals, silicones, glass fibre and LCD glass
- **Jewellery**: high-end bridal and fashion jewellery, especially in China, the US, Japan and India
- **Investment**: bars, coins and physically backed ETFs
- **Hydrogen**: catalysts in PEM fuel cells and electrolysers, as well as emerging e-fuel and green ammonia value chains

### Demand Growth

PGMs in autocatalysts (mostly Pt + Pd + Rh) rose from **2.2 Moz in 1990 to 13.8 Moz in 2019**, a ~6x increase driven mainly by tighter emissions standards rather than vehicle sales alone. Pt's share within autocatalyst PGMs fell in the 1990s–2000s as Pd gained share in gasoline engines, but is now rising again as automakers re-optimise loadings.

Aggregate Pt demand (all sectors) has risen from low single-digit Moz in the early 1990s to over **8 Moz in recent years** (~8.12 Moz forecast for 2024). The composition has changed more than the total:

| Sector     | Share of Total Pt Demand | Trend                                                      |
| ---------- | ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Automotive | ~40–45%                  | Rose from minority share in 1990 to largest segment today  |
| Industrial | ~26–36%                  | Growing ~2x faster than global GDP since 2013              |
| Jewellery  | ~23–30%                  | Rose to ~2.1 Moz by 2019, stagnated, recovering post-Covid |
| Investment | ~10–20%                  | Highly volatile year to year                               |

### Future Demand Drivers

- **Automotive**: BEVs eventually reduce catalyst demand, but tougher emissions (Euro 7, China 6b) and high-PGM hybrid catalysts support loadings through the 2020s. Pt-for-Pd substitution in gasoline catalysts is still ongoing.
- **Hydrogen**: CME and WPIC estimate hydrogen end-markets could account for **~11% of total Pt demand (~900 koz) by 2030**, up from ~40 koz in 2023. Longer-term projections suggest hydrogen could become the **largest single Pt end-use by the late 2030s** (~3.5 Moz/year).
- **Jewellery**: modest low-single-digit growth, with WPIC seeing jewellery demand rising ~2% in 2025 to a six-year high.
- **Industrial & medical**: steady growth in glass, chemical, electronics and medical uses.

WPIC's 2025 "Platinum Essentials" expects total Pt demand to rise slightly from **7.95 Moz (2024e) to ~8.0 Moz in 2029**.

### Reserves

From USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025:

- **World platinum mine production (2024e):** 170,000 kg (~5.47 Moz)
- **World PGM reserves:** >81,000 t, with South Africa at 63,000 t, Russia at 16,000 t, Zimbabwe at 1,200 t

Because Pt comes from the same ore bodies as other PGMs, its effective reserve life depends on co-product economics and future cut-off grades rather than a separate Pt reserve number. Recycling already provides **~20–25% of platinum supply** and is expected to grow, extending effective availability beyond static R/P ratios.

### Price Outlook

Pt traded mostly below gold and, from ~2016–2022, **below palladium**, despite frequent physical deficits. USGS data show average Pt prices around 886–1,094 USD/oz over 2020–2022, then ~973 USD/oz in 2023 and ~950 USD/oz estimated for 2024.

WPIC and Johnson Matthey show **structural deficits** since 2023, linked to constrained South African output and robust auto, industrial and investment demand.

| Source                | Forecast                                                                      |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| World Bank (Oct 2025) | Pt prices up ~29% in 2025, +4% in 2026, +2% in 2027 vs 2024                   |
| WPIC                  | Average deficits of ~348 koz/year from 2027–2030                              |
| Market consensus      | Tight to 2030; elevated and potentially rising prices if hydrogen accelerates |

<callout variant="note" title="Platinum outlook">Platinum looks structurally better supported on both the fundamentals (deficits, hydrogen) and reserve-risk side than most other PGMs over the next decade.</callout>

## Palladium

### Uses

Before 1990, Pd was used heavily in **electronics** and as a cheaper Pt substitute in jewellery; autocatalyst use was smaller but growing. In the 1990s, Pd became dominant in gasoline catalysts because it was cheaper and well-suited to high-temperature gasoline exhaust.

Core uses today:

- **Automotive gasoline autocatalysts** — currently **~80% of total palladium demand**
- **Electronics**: multi-layer ceramic capacitors, connectors, some plating
- **Chemical catalysts**: hydrogenation, carbon-carbon coupling in fine chemicals and pharma
- **Dental alloys and jewellery**: declining segments
- **Investment**: coins and bars, but relatively small

By the late 1990s and early 2000s, Pd demand frequently exceeded mine supply, with Russian stock drawdowns filling the gap. A 2000 supply disruption spiked prices from ~200 to >1,000 USD/oz in months.

### Demand Growth

Autocatalyst demand became the dominant Pd end-use by the mid-1990s, overtaking electronics. By the late 2010s, autocatalysts accounted for ~80% of total Pd demand. Johnson Matthey and SFA Oxford document **multi-fold growth in Pd demand from 1990 to its peak around 2019**, driven by rising global gasoline car sales, ever-tighter emissions standards, Pd-heavy catalyst formulations and limited thrifting due to durability constraints.

From 2012 to 2024, **the palladium market ran persistent annual deficits**, closed by increasing mine output (mainly Russia and South Africa), higher recycling from spent autocatalysts and drawdown of above-ground stocks.

### Future Demand

- **Automotive**: WPIC expects Pd demand to decline from **~10.3 Moz in 2024 to ~9.7 Moz in 2029** (-1% CAGR), mostly due to BEV penetration and substitution by Pt in gasoline catalysts. Still, as long as ICE and hybrid sales remain high (WPIC expects BEVs at ~29% of global LV market by 2030), palladium remains heavily used.
- **Industrial and chemical**: modest growth or flat; lower prices vs peak years could encourage renewed use of Pd catalysts where base-metal substitutes were previously adopted.
- **Hydrogen**: Pd is not a main hydrogen catalyst metal, but hydrogen growth indirectly supports Pd because platinum diverted into hydrogen applications encourages more Pd-for-Pt substitution in autocatalysts.

### Reserves

USGS gives combined PGM reserves and production; Pd is not separated. World palladium mine production (2024e) is **190,000 kg (~6.11 Moz)**. Pd and Pt share the same ore bodies — the ratio of Pt:Pd in Bushveld and Norilsk ores is geologically constrained. If ~40–45% of PGM output by mass is Pd and reserves are 81,000 t of PGM, then palladium's notional reserve life is of the same order (~150–200 years) at current output.

Johnson Matthey and USGS emphasise **operational constraints** (South African power, Russian geopolitics) as the real near- and medium-term bottleneck, not geological depletion.

### Price Outlook

Pd prices exploded from sub-500 USD/oz in the early 2000s to peaks above **2,500 USD/oz in 2019–2021** during the period of chronic deficits. USGS reports average annual Pd prices at ~2,205 USD/oz (2020), 2,419 (2021), 2,134 (2022), 1,352 (2023) and an estimated **980 USD/oz in 2024** — a sharp retreat from peak.

| Source                        | Forecast                                                                    |
| ----------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Johnson Matthey (2025)        | Market moves from persistent deficit to balance in 2025, surplus risk later |
| WPIC                          | Deficits persist to ~2027, surpluses emerge from 2028 onward                |
| SFA "Peak Palladium"          | Autocatalyst Pd demand to fall ~20% by 2030 from its 2019 peak              |
| BullionVault (2026 consensus) | Pd price expectations broadly in the 1,100–1,500 USD/oz range               |

<callout variant="warning" title="Palladium outlook">Palladium is transitioning from a tight, deficit-driven bull market to a more balanced or surplus market. Medium-term price risk is skewed to the downside unless ICE regulations tighten faster than EV penetration.</callout>

## Ruthenium

### Uses

Ru was a **minor alloying and contact material** through most of the 20th century. Demand accelerated with the growth of HDDs and miniaturised electronics from the 1990s onward, and again with high-density enterprise storage and AI/cloud infrastructure.

Key uses today:

- **Electronics & electrical**: thin films in HDD magnetic layers (enabling high-density storage), chip resistors, capacitors, thick-film pastes, electrochemical devices and supercapacitors
- **Chemical & electrochemical catalysts**: hydrogenation, ammonia synthesis derivatives, caprolactam (nylon-6 precursor) and other specialty chemicals — especially significant in China
- **Other**: wear-resistant electrical contacts, some jewellery alloys and emerging fuel cell/electrolyser catalyst roles

### Demand Growth

From 1990 to today, **Ru demand has grown by several multiples** from a small base, mainly tracking electronics and data-centre infrastructure.

| Sector                             | Share of Ru Demand | Trend                                          |
| ---------------------------------- | ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Electronics & electrical           | ~45–54%            | Rising with semiconductor and HDD complexity   |
| Chemical/electrochemical catalysts | ~20–25%            | Includes caprolactam and specialty chemicals   |
| Automotive & other                 | ~25–35%            | Contacts, alloying, nascent hydrogen catalysts |

Johnson Matthey's 2025 PGM Market Report notes that **ruthenium demand hit an all-time high in chemicals in 2024** and rebounded strongly in HDDs. The Ru market was in deficit in 2024 despite sales from producer stocks, and demand is expected to rise ~2% in 2025 with a **deep deficit unless further inventory is drawn down**.

Ru prices surged **~80% in 2025 to ~830 USD/oz**, surpassing the percentage rises in Pt (+45%) and Pd (+24%), driven by intense HDD demand for AI data centres, concerns about South African supply (80% of global Ru output) and stockpiling by OEMs.

### Reserves

USGS does not report separate Ru reserves — only combined PGM reserves. By tonnage, Ru represents only a few percent of the PGM content of Bushveld-type ores. Johnson Matthey treats Ru availability as **co-product-constrained**, not geologically scarce. Given PGM reserves of ~81,000 t and annual Ru production of the order of a few tens of tonnes, a simplistic R/P ratio would be well over a century, but **effective availability is tied to Pt/Pd mine plans and refinery routing**.

Producer Ru stocks and the ability to tweak refining "splits" between metals have historically buffered the market. Recent deficits are more about low prices discouraging recovery and inventory depletion than ore exhaustion.

### Price Outlook

There are no widely published long-term Ru price curves. Johnson Matthey identifies a **deep market deficit for Ru in 2025** absent further inventory drawdown. With AI/HDD demand and hydrogen-related catalysis, Ru prices may remain **elevated and volatile**, especially given highly concentrated South African supply, lack of exchange trading and stockpiling by OEMs.

Third-party market research forecasts **Ru market CAGRs of ~5–6% in value to 2032–2035**, implying moderate volume growth but strong price sensitivity.

<callout variant="note" title="Ruthenium outlook">Ruthenium faces tight, structurally growing demand in electronics and catalysts, with availability constrained by its co-product nature and South African risk — expect continued price volatility and episodic squeezes rather than a smooth trend.</callout>

## Iridium

### Uses

Before hydrogen became a theme, Ir demand was relatively flat, dominated by high-temperature alloys and niche chemical uses. Over the last decade, **PEM electrolysers and fuel-cell research** have significantly raised its profile.

Key uses today:

- **High-temperature and high-wear components**: crucibles for single-crystal growth (e.g. sapphire for LEDs and optics), turbine and jet engine parts, spark plugs and ignition components
- **Electrochemical & chemical catalysts**: particularly as an **anode catalyst in PEM water electrolysers**, as well as in some specialty chemical and petrochemical processes
- **Electronics**: electrodes, contacts, OLED phosphors and other niche applications

### Demand Growth

Johnson Matthey's 2025 PGM Market Report notes that **industrial applications dominate iridium consumption**, with chemicals and electrochemistry as key segments. In 2024, demand improved modestly due to lower prices and robust chemicals/electrochemical purchases. **Green-energy applications (electrolysers, fuel cells) were the main growth driver** in electrochemical uses.

Third-party market studies estimate the global iridium market at ~1.2–2.7 billion USD in 2023–2024 with a projected CAGR of ~5–6% to 2030–2033, driven chiefly by electronics, automotive (spark plugs, catalysts) and hydrogen fuel cells and electrolysers.

A 2024 scientific review on PGMs and hydrogen projects that by **2050**, consumption of Pt and Ir for fuel-cell vehicles and PEM electrolysis could reach **~143 t Pt and ~38 t Ir per year** — well above today's iridium production if no thrifting or substitution occurs. Current PGM recycling is ~26% of supply and must increase, alongside thrifting, to avoid supply being a bottleneck.

### Reserves

As with Ru, USGS does not split out iridium reserves. Ir is the **rarest of the PGMs in ore** and typically the smallest fraction of PGM output by mass. Johnson Matthey and specialist white papers argue that there is enough Ir geologically to support large-scale hydrogen deployment, but **only if** Ir loading per kW in electrolysers continues to fall, recycling rates increase sharply and manufacturing focuses on designs that maximise Ir utilisation.

<callout variant="important" title="Iridium availability">While headline R/P ratios for PGMs look comfortable, iridium is the most likely PGM to face technology-driven "availability constraints" rather than outright geological depletion, especially in aggressive hydrogen scenarios.</callout>

### Price Outlook

USGS shows average Ir prices jumping from ~1,634 USD/oz (2020) to >5,158 (2021), then moderating but still very high at ~4,582 (2022), 4,673 (2023) and an estimated **4,800 USD/oz in 2024**. These prices are orders of magnitude above historical norms, reflecting tiny market size and hydrogen expectations.

Johnson Matthey expects the Ir market to be **roughly balanced in 2025**, with slight increases in primary supply matching stable demand. Consulting houses highlight supply concentration in South African and Russian PGM circuits, hydrogen as a key demand growth driver and intense R&D into Ir-thrifty and Ir-free technologies which could cap very long-term price upside.

The most likely outcome is **continued volatility around a high price level** with structural demand growth moderated by thrifting and substitution.

## Reserves & Reserve Life

Using USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025 and NRCan cross-checks:

| Country         | PGM Reserves (tonnes) | Share    |
| --------------- | --------------------- | -------- |
| South Africa    | 63,000                | ~78%     |
| Russia          | 16,000                | ~20%     |
| Zimbabwe        | 1,200                 | ~1.5%    |
| United States   | 820                   | ~1%      |
| Canada          | 310                   | <1%      |
| **World total** | **>81,000**           | **100%** |

Global PGM mine production averaged **447 t/year over 2010–2023**. In 2024, approximately 13.1 Moz was mined (~407 t), plus ~4.6 Moz recycled. Breaking down the 2024 estimates: Pt at 170 t and Pd at 190 t.

**Reserve life (R/P) ballpark:**

- 81,000 t / 447 t = **~181 years** (using 2010–23 average)
- 81,000 t / 407 t = **~199 years** (using 2024 mine supply)

For Pt and Pd together (~360 t/year mine output in 2024), effective R/P is also on the order of ~200 years, but highly sensitive to future prices, mine plan changes, shifts in relative metal prices (which change recovery emphasis) and recycling intensity.

For Ru and Ir, individual tonnage in reserves is small but not the primary constraint. **Co-product status and refinery choice** drive flows. For Ir specifically, future hydrogen demand (tens of tonnes per year in some scenarios) could approach or exceed today's global output, making technology and recycling the gating factor.

<callout variant="note" title="Reserves in context">At current production, PGMs collectively have ~180–200 years of reserves — but this is a moving target. Price, technology, recycling and policy will all change it. From an investor or strategic-sourcing perspective, the short- to medium-term constraints are operational and geopolitical, not geological.</callout>

## Sector Growth Trajectories

### Automotive

PGM use in autocatalysts rose from **2.2 Moz (1990) to 13.8 Moz (2019)**. PGMs now account for ~60% of total PGM end-use when combining Pt, Pd and Rh across light-duty, heavy-duty and hybrids. The 2020–2024 period saw Covid-related production drops, then recovery to ~89–90 million global light-vehicle sales in 2024, alongside strict new emissions standards (Euro 6d/7, China 6, India BS VI) increasing PGM loadings.

Looking forward, WPIC and SFA expect ICE + hybrid vehicle sales to remain significant through at least the 2030s, but with **gradually declining PGM demand** as BEVs take share and thrifting continues. For Pt, substitution for Pd in gasoline engines offsets some of this decline; for Pd, it compounds it.

### Electronics & Data Infrastructure

Global electronics and data-centre growth have been strong secular drivers for Ru and, to a lesser extent, Ir. HDDs and cloud storage are a key ruthenium consumer — AI build-out in 2025 is estimated to raise HDD sales by ~16%, supporting Ru demand. Semiconductor miniaturisation and advanced components need Ru and Ir in resistors, contacts and specialty layers.

Most forecasts to 2030–2035 see **Ru and Ir markets growing at ~5–6% CAGR in value**, with volume growth somewhat lower but still positive.

### Hydrogen & Green Energy

WPIC and CME estimate hydrogen applications for Pt rising from **~40 koz in 2023 to ~900 koz (~11% of total Pt demand) by 2030**. Looking further out to 2040–2050:

- **Platinum** demand from hydrogen could reach **3.5 Moz/year**, becoming Pt's largest single end-market
- **Iridium** demand for PEM electrolysis and fuel cells could reach **~38 t/year by 2050**, which is large relative to current production levels

These figures are scenario-based, but collectively they frame **hydrogen as a key long-run growth engine for Pt and Ir**, with some emerging roles for Ru in catalysts.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Platinum** — demand has grown modestly since 1990, but its mix has shifted strongly toward automotive and now hydrogen. It is in **structural deficit** with long reserve life but significant supply concentration risk. Most medium-term outlooks are constructively bullish on both demand (hydrogen, substitution) and price.

2. **Palladium** — was the "star" PGM of the 2010s, fuelled by gasoline autocatalysts and chronic deficits. As BEVs grow and Pt substitutes back into gasoline catalysts, Pd demand is expected to **decline modestly** and the market to move into surplus. Price risk over 5–10 years is skewed to the downside absent new demand sources.

3. **Ruthenium** — critical for electronics (HDDs, chip resistors) and certain chemical catalysts; demand has grown from negligible levels in 1990 to a structurally important niche today. Market is small, illiquid and co-product constrained — expect **volatile but structurally supported pricing** as AI, cloud and hydrogen grow.

4. **Iridium** — tiny market but strategically key for PEM electrolysers and some fuel cells. Geological reserves are ample in a PGM sense, but availability is constrained by co-product status and processing capacity. Long-run viability of the hydrogen economy is tightly bound to **Ir thrifting and recycling**; prices are likely to remain high and volatile.

5. **Reserves and longevity** — at current production, PGMs collectively have ~180–200 years of reserves, but the short- to medium-term constraints are **operational and geopolitical, not geological**.

## Supply Concentration Risk

The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) of reserve concentration rises sharply as you move from gold and silver to the rarer PGMs. While gold reserves are distributed across 30+ countries, iridium reserves are meaningful in only five. For industries that depend on these metals — automotive catalysts, green hydrogen, electronics — diversifying supply remains a strategic priority.

### Reserve and market estimates

- **U.S. Geological Survey (USGS), _Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025_** — Gold, Silver, and Platinum-Group Metals chapters. Published January 2025 by the U.S. Department of the Interior. Country-level reserve estimates for Au, Ag, Pt, Pd and PGMs as a group. Primary source for all reserve figures used in this map.

- **World Platinum Investment Council (WPIC), _Platinum Essentials 2025_** — Five-year supply-demand forecasts for platinum, palladium and other PGMs. Source for demand sector breakdowns, deficit/surplus projections and hydrogen demand estimates.
- **Natural Resources Canada (NRCan)** — Cross-check for global PGM reserves (81,330 t) and 2024 total PGM supply (17.7 Moz mined + recycled).
- **World Bank, _Commodity Markets Outlook_ (October 2025)** — Price forecasts for precious metals including platinum.
- **SFA Oxford, _Peak Palladium_ research** — Analysis of long-term palladium demand decline scenarios and autocatalyst substitution trends.
- **Afriforesight (2025)** — PGM price analysis and South African supply risk commentary, particularly for ruthenium market dynamics.

### Mine locations

- **S&P Global Market Intelligence, _Metals & Mining database_** — Mine-level coordinates, status (active/development/closed), ownership and production data for all six metals.
- **Company annual reports and technical disclosures** — NI 43-101 (Canada), JORC (Australia/international) and SEC S-K 1300 compliant technical reports for individual mine sites, used to verify coordinates and operational status.

- **Esri (World Dark Gray Base)** — Dark-themed basemap tiles.
